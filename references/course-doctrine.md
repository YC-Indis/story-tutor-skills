# Course doctrine

Use this reference as the conceptual spine. Apply it to the creator's evidence; do not repeat it as a lecture unless asked.

## 1. The governing idea

Filmmaking combines storytelling and audiovisual execution to make viewers enter, care about, and remember an experience. A beautiful image is not automatically a meaningful image. Every creative choice should be traceable to:

1. what the viewer must understand;
2. what the viewer should feel;
3. what the viewer should notice or anticipate;
4. what change the choice helps reveal.

Learning becomes capability only through small completed projects, evidence, outside feedback, reflection, and targeted practice. Prefer a finished bounded film over an ambitious unfinished one.

## 2. Story discovery and focus

Build a story around four interacting elements:

- **Task:** what the character is trying to do now;
- **Conflict:** what forces effort, uncertainty, choice, or sacrifice;
- **Transformation:** what changes externally and internally;
- **Vulnerability:** why the outcome matters to this person and why viewers can care.

Find stories in personal experience, people, environments, and issues, then narrow to one angle. The angle is a deletion rule: material that does not serve it is context debt, not automatic value.

Use a one-sentence story test:

> A specific person wants X now, but Y creates a meaningful risk or choice, leading toward Z change.

Distinguish topic from story. “Running a marathon” is a topic; a person's attempt, obstacle, decision, and changed relationship to failure can become a story.

## 3. Storyteller responsibility

Manage information so the viewer can orient without being over-explained. Establish only the context needed for the next meaningful question. Preserve ambiguity when it creates fair anticipation; remove it when it causes accidental confusion.

Choose the storyteller's position deliberately:

- outside observer;
- participant-protagonist;
- reflective narrator;
- investigator or guide.

Do not enter the story merely for screen time. Enter when personal stakes, access, responsibility, or point of view materially changes the experience.

## 4. Structure, tension, and hooks

Structure is controlled change in expectation. A practical six-part default is:

```text
Hook → Context → Setup → Progression → Finale → Reflection
```

Treat it as a starting model, not a rigid formula.

- **Hook:** pose a credible question, contrast, risk, or promise.
- **Context:** give the minimum information needed to care correctly.
- **Setup:** establish the task, rules, plan, and stakes.
- **Progression:** alternate attempts, discoveries, obstacles, decisions, and updated strategies.
- **Finale:** deliver the highest consequence, decisive action, or answer.
- **Reflection:** compress the experienced change into one memorable meaning.

Tension grows from expectation + uncertainty + investment. Escalation does not require louder music or faster cutting; it can come from fewer options, greater cost, delayed information, closer emotional access, or a harder choice.

A hook is a contract. Do not imply a payoff the film cannot deliver.

## 5. Show, do not merely tell

Use image and sound as evidence, not decoration. Decide what information is best carried by:

- visible action or behavior;
- environmental detail;
- reaction and silence;
- A-roll speech;
- voice-over;
- captured or archival B-roll;
- graphics or animation;
- production sound, ambience, music, or designed sound.

Avoid saying exactly what an image already proves unless the repetition adds irony, emotional contrast, precision, or accessibility. Let the external journey be seen and use speech/voice-over for missing context, internal change, time compression, or reflection.

## 6. Physical and emotional journeys

Track both lines:

| Beat | Physical state/action | Emotional state | Pressure/choice | Evidence |
|---|---|---|---|---|

They should affect each other. External progress without internal consequence feels procedural; internal reflection without action feels asserted.

## 7. Research and writing

Use the writing sequence:

```text
Research → Outline → Write → Edit
```

Research for knowledge, evidence, and inspiration. Use a Consume → Dump → Cluster → Organize loop, and stop when the material can support decisions rather than endless collection.

Outline causally rather than listing events. Give major phases story-specific names. For every beat, identify:

- what changes;
- why this beat follows the previous one;
- what question or pressure it creates next;
- what image, sound, speech, or event can prove it.

Use scripting levels according to uncertainty:

1. **Word-for-word:** definitions, precise narration, designed presentations.
2. **Bullet points:** natural delivery with factual anchors.
3. **Questions:** interviews or interactions driven by another person.
4. **Improvisation/event reservation:** unpredictable moments that cannot be honestly pre-written.

Scripts may change before shooting, during production, and after footage review. Write final voice-over against actual A-roll and B-roll, not imagined footage.

Edit prose in separate passes: order and prerequisites, mainline deletion, sentence economy, read-aloud naturalness, formatting, factual/grammar check, and outside-reader clarity.

## 8. Voice-over doctrine

Use voice-over only when it performs a necessary job:

- supplies missing context;
- connects events that footage cannot connect naturally;
- expresses the internal journey while images show the external one;
- compresses time;
- frames a visual passage with a viewing purpose;
- lands a reflection;
- hands one sequence to the next.

Leave room for sync sound and unmediated moments. Test every line against visible evidence. Maintain a claim ledger for factual or memory-based statements.

## 9. Shoot organization

Progress from cinematic shooting to intentional shooting to organized shooting. Organize so visual intentions are defined before the expensive moment of capture.

Translate the project through these levels:

```text
Video → Chapter → Scene → Shot → Frame
```

Plan scenes and shots around their story functions. Examine visual elements in three groups:

- **Setting:** location, lighting, color;
- **Character:** actor, outfit, action;
- **Camera:** type, settings, focal length, shot size/composition, angle, movement, focus.

Use the planning chain:

```text
Mood → Location → Light → Shot list → Schedule
```

Prioritize shots as Must / Should / Could. A viable shot list covers geography, main action, decision/reaction, meaningful details, entrances/exits or transitions, sync sound, wild track, and room tone.

## 10. Gear and shooting

Choose gear from story function, position, constraints, safety, and reliability. Do not use ownership or novelty as the primary criterion. Evaluate benefits and limitations of cameras, lenses, filters, stabilization, phones, action/360 cameras, drones, lighting, and audio in the actual production context.

Image making follows:

```text
Story beat
→ Viewer psychology
→ Aesthetic rules
→ Visual elements
→ Shot purpose/size/angle/composition/movement
→ Coverage/continuity/audio
→ Lighting and production constraints
```

On set, troubleshoot in this order:

1. safety and consent;
2. whether image and sound are recording;
3. whether exposure, focus, sound, and action are readable;
4. whether the shot serves the intended beat;
5. whether the footage will connect and cover the scene;
6. aesthetic quality;
7. efficiency and whether to move on.

For unpredictable events, capture an event cycle: objective → attempt → reaction → updated understanding or strategy. Do not let a challenge rule override health, law, privacy, or consent.

## 11. Editing decisions

Editing writes the film again through three elemental decisions:

- **Selection:** which moment or evidence is shown or withheld;
- **Chronology:** how adjacency creates time, causality, comparison, or surprise;
- **Duration:** how long importance, emotion, comprehension, and energy are allowed to develop.

Evaluate them through three priority levels:

### Story

- **Context:** can viewers understand the task, pressure, causality, and change?
- **Emotion:** do selected behaviors, reactions, words, images, sounds, and pauses support a clear emotional north star?
- **Rhythm:** do timing patterns and changes serve comprehension and feeling?

### Engagement

- **Diversity:** does context, emotion, or rhythm evolve before habituation takes over?
- **Authenticity:** does the cut preserve credible behavior, time, space, sound, and character intent?
- **Originality:** does a creative device solve a real exposition or experience problem instead of copying a template?

### Flow

- **Continuity:** check narrative, performance, temporal, spatial, eyeline, color, audio, and graphic continuity.
- **Eye trace:** guide, follow, or deliberately reset attention across cuts.
- **Aesthetic:** make technical, visual, audio, graphic, color, and delivery details feel coherent.

Story is the prerequisite. Engagement must not damage it. Flow removes unintentional friction and unifies the result.

## 12. Story versus retention

Treat story-led and retention-led editing as a spectrum. Choose according to audience, platform, subject, desired experience, and success metric.

Story-led editing can deepen thought, emotion, connection, and universality but may require more patience. Retention-led editing can lower entry friction and compress information but risks overstimulation, joyless attention, manipulation, and shallow narrative.

Use a style curve across the film rather than one global speed. A hook may reduce entry friction; a vulnerable scene may need stillness; a procedural middle may need compression; a payoff may need time to land.

## 13. Feedback and learning

Choose feedback people by what they can observe: self, target-like viewer, filmmaker, or client. Ask at a stage where their answer is actionable. During viewing, capture raw reactions and do not defend the cut.

Separate the problem signal from the suggested solution. Evaluate feedback by recurrence, viewer type, alignment with intention, evidence, severity, dependencies, cost, and reversibility.

After delivery, preserve an evidence book: versions, decisions, feedback, before/after examples, stage results, time spent, failures, and lessons. Choose only 1–3 improvements for the next project. Build mastery through progressive overload, leading indicators, useful relationships, and bounded career experiments.
